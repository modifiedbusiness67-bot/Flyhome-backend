# AOTEAROA BEFORE: Māori Life in 1600
## Narration Script for Faceless-Video Documentary

**Total Runtime**: 6:45 (approximately 1,350 words)  
**Language**: English with intentional te reo Māori integration  
**Subtitle Format**: Bilingual (English + te reo Māori key terms)

## ACT 1: WHAKAPAPA & SETTLEMENT
**[0:00 - 1:15]**

Over six hundred years before the arrival of Europeans, a great journey across the vast Pacific Ocean brought our ancestors to these islands. 

They came in great waaka—double-hulled canoes—navigating by the stars, the ocean swells, and the knowledge passed down through generations. *[VISUAL: Waaka approaching coastline at dawn]*

These were the Polynesian voyagers. And when they arrived at what they called Aotearoa—the Land of the Long White Cloud—they found a land untouched by human hands. Vast forests. Abundant wildlife. Rivers teeming with kai moana, seafood that sustained entire iwi, entire tribes.

They settled. They explored. They learned this new land's rhythms and seasons. And over centuries, they built a civilization uniquely their own. *[VISUAL: Iwi settlements spreading across North and South islands]*

By 1600, Aotearoa belonged entirely to Māori. 

We had developed complex societies. Iwi governed their own territories—Ngāpuhi in the north, Ngāti Tūwharetoa in the central plateau, Ngāi Tahu in the south. Each iwi had its own identity, its own stories, its own connection to the land.

Our whānau—our families—were the center of everything. *[VISUAL: Extended whānau gathered together]* Whānau meant not just relatives by blood, but a kinship that bound us to each other and to the whenua, the land itself.

We were tangata whenua. People of the land. And we knew no other way of living than this: in harmony with Aotearoa, speaking only te reo Māori, our language—the language of our tīpuna, our ancestors, stretching back through countless generations.

## ACT 2: KAI & MAHI
**[1:15 - 3:15]**

Our survival depended on understanding the land and the sea in ways that went deeper than mere survival. It was a sacred relationship.

*[VISUAL: Seasonal cycles montage]*

In spring, we cultivated our gardens. The kūmara—the sweet potato brought from Hawaiki by our ancestors—grew in carefully tended soil. We also grew taro, another precious kai whenua, food from the land. We understood rotation, soil quality, when to plant and when to harvest. This was mahi—our work—but it was also knowledge, passed from tūpuna to tamariki, from grandparents to children.

*[VISUAL: Men hunting in forests, women gathering]* 

When hunting season came, our men tracked moa, the great flightless birds, and deer through dense forests. They laid snares, used spears with precision honed over generations. Every part of the animal was used—nothing wasted. The bones became tools. The hides became clothing. Nothing was taken without gratitude.

*[VISUAL: Coastal scenes—people gathering kai moana]*

But our most abundant food came from the moana, the ocean. We fished with nets woven from native flax. We dove for kina and pāua. We gathered pipi and tuatua from the shores. The kai moana sustained us. It was our supermarket, our pharmacy, our security.

*[VISUAL: Whare construction, pā fortifications]*

And we built homes worthy of our mahi. Our whare were constructed with precision and care. Wooden frames lashed together with materials from the land. Thatch roofs that shed rain and kept warmth inside. Inside the whare, a central pūtātā—a fire—where whānau gathered to cook, to talk, to share stories.

Some of us built pā—fortified villages positioned on hilltops overlooking valleys and waterways. *[VISUAL: Aerial view of pā construction]* These were not just defensive structures. They were centers of community. From the pā, we could see our crops. We could watch for danger. We could gather together.

Every season had its own rhythm. Every mahi had its purpose. We lived not just *on* the land—we lived *with* the land, understanding its patterns, its gifts, its requirements.

There was no separation between us and Aotearoa. We were of this place. It was of us.

## ACT 3: CULTURE & WHAKAPAPA
**[3:15 - 4:45]**

To be Māori was to carry your whakapapa—your genealogy—in your heart and on your tongue. *[VISUAL: Whakapapa scroll unrolling, or genealogy tree visualization]*

Your whakapapa connected you to your iwi, to your ancestors, to the gods themselves. To recite one's whakapapa was to claim identity. It was to say: "I know who I am. I know where I come from."

The tohunga—our experts and priests—were the keepers of this knowledge. *[VISUAL: Tohunga performing karakia by a pūtātā]* A tohunga tohunga whakairo was a master carver. A tohunga tapua was a spiritual expert. A tohunga kōrero was a keeper of stories. Each had spent years, sometimes decades, mastering their craft and knowledge.

Knowledge in our culture was sacred. It was not written in books—it was carried in memory, in practice, in the careful teaching of the next generation. This is why the kōrero—the talking, the discussion—was so important. *[VISUAL: Whānau gathered, elder speaking]*

When important decisions needed to be made, we gathered for hui. We sat together. We listened. We spoke. We debated. We reached consensus through the power of collective wisdom.

And we expressed our identity through the arts. *[VISUAL: Weaving, carving, traditional crafts]*

Our tohunga whakairo carved intricate patterns into wood and bone. Each pattern had meaning. Each spiral and line told a story or represented a genealogy. The whakairo wasn't decoration—it was history frozen in wood.

Our wāhine—our women—performed the ancient art of raranga, weaving. With flax from our land, they created baskets, mats, and clothing of stunning beauty and durability. Patterns were handed down through generations. Each design carried meaning.

And we gathered to perform the haka—the traditional dance that expressed our power, our unity, our fierce pride as Māori. *[VISUAL: Haka performance, warriors in traditional dress]* The haka was not entertainment. It was ceremony. It was the expression of our collective wairua—our spirit.

We also sang waiata—our songs—which carried our stories. Through waiata, we remembered great victories. We mourned our dead. We celebrated love and whānau. The waiata kept our history alive.

Our spiritual beliefs ran deep. We believed in atua—gods—who inhabited every part of nature. Tangaroa ruled the moana. Tāne guarded the forests. Papatūānuku was the earth mother. Every mountain, every river, every tree had its own mauri—its own spiritual essence.

We performed karakia—prayers and incantations—to acknowledge these forces and seek their protection. Before we hunted, we performed karakia. Before we planted, karakia. These were not superstitions—they were expressions of respect and gratitude for the life we took and the gifts we received.

This was our culture. This was our way.

## ACT 4: TE REO MĀORI - THE LIVING LANGUAGE
**[4:45 - 6:45]**

But at the heart of all of this—at the very center of Māori identity—was te reo Māori. Our language. *[VISUAL: Māori words appearing on screen: tangata, whenua, moana, maunga, wairua]*

Te reo Māori was not just a way to communicate. It was a way of seeing the world.

In English, we might say "the ocean." In te reo Māori, we say "te moana"—and that phrase carries within it centuries of relationship, of respect, of knowledge about the ocean. *[VISUAL: Ocean imagery]*

When we said "tangata whenua"—people of the land—those three words held an entire philosophy. We were not separate from the land. We belonged to it. We were part of it.

When we spoke of our "tīpuna"—our ancestors—we invoked their presence. They were not distant history. They were with us, guiding us, speaking through us.

*[VISUAL: Montage of whānau, daily life, cultural practices, spiritual moments]*

Our language encoded our values. 

"Manaakitanga" means hospitality and care for others—but it goes deeper. It means recognizing the mauri, the essential life force, in every person.

"Kaitiakitanga" means guardianship of the land—but it's not ownership. It's a sacred responsibility to protect and nurture what has been entrusted to us.

"Whanaungatanga" speaks to relationships and kinship—but it extends beyond family. It's about the connections that bind us all together.

Every word in te reo Māori carried this depth. When you spoke our language, you were speaking the worldview of your ancestors. You were participating in thousands of years of Polynesian wisdom.

*[VISUAL: Children learning te reo, hands making shapes for words, elders teaching]*

Before 1600, every Māori child learned te reo Māori as their first and only language. It was the water we swam in. The air we breathed. The voice of our tīpuna speaking through us.

We recited karakia in te reo Māori. We sang waiata in te reo Māori. We debated in hui using te reo Māori. We told our stories, our histories, our genealogies in te reo Māori.

*[VISUAL: Sunset over Aotearoa, whānau gathered around fire]*

In 1600, Aotearoa belonged to us. Our iwi governed these lands. Our whānau thrived. Our culture flourished. Our language filled the air from the northern tip of the North Island to the southern reaches of the South Island.

We were free. We were whole. We were Māori.

Te reo Māori was the voice of that world. It was the expression of our identity, our values, our connection to each other and to this sacred land.

*[VISUAL: Stars appearing over Aotearoa, suggesting timelessness and continuity]*

This is the Aotearoa that existed before colonisation. This is the world our ancestors knew. This is the language they spoke. This is the legacy we carry within us today.

Because even though te reo Māori was nearly lost, it was never truly silenced. And today, as we revive our language, we reclaim our identity. We honor our ancestors. We speak as our tīpuna spoke.

Kia ora. Thank you for listening to our story.
