## 🎯 VOCAL DELIVERY GUIDELINES

### Tone & Pacing
- **Speak clearly** but naturally — not overly theatrical
- **Moderate pace**: Not rushed, but engaging (not slow/dragging)
- **Warm delivery**: As if speaking to someone you respect and care about
- **Contemplative**: Especially during Act 4 (language/closing)
- **Respect in voice**: Honor the cultural subject matter

### Breathing & Pauses
- Take natural breaths between sentences
- Pause slightly at bullet points (indicated with `[PAUSE]` in script)
- Let important concepts breathe — don't rush key moments
- Pause before te reo Māori words to give them emphasis

### Te Reo Māori Pronunciation
- **See pronunciation guide below** for all Māori words
- Take extra care with genealogy/spiritual terms
- Slight emphasis on Māori words (but not exaggerated)
- Let the words land naturally as part of the story

## 📖 NARRATION SCRIPT WITH RECORDING NOTES

### ACT 1: WHAKAPAPA & SETTLEMENT
**[0:00 - 1:15] — Tone: Reverent, grounded, storytelling**

Over six hundred years before the arrival of Europeans, a great journey across the vast Pacific Ocean brought our ancestors to these islands. 

They came in great **waaka** [PAUSE] — double-hulled canoes — navigating by the stars, the ocean swells, and the knowledge passed down through generations. 

These were the Polynesian voyagers. And when they arrived at what they called **Aotearoa** [PAUSE] — the Land of the Long White Cloud — they found a land untouched by human hands. Vast forests. Abundant wildlife. Rivers teeming with **kai moana** [PAUSE], seafood that sustained entire **iwi**, entire tribes.

They settled. They explored. They learned this new land's rhythms and seasons. And over centuries, they built a civilization uniquely their own. [PAUSE — visual moment]

By 1600, Aotearoa belonged entirely to Māori. 

We had developed complex societies. **Iwi** governed their own territories — **Ngāpuhi** in the north, **Ngāti Tūwharetoa** in the central plateau, **Ngāi Tahu** in the south. Each iwi had its own identity, its own stories, its own connection to the land.

Our **whānau** [PAUSE] — our families — were the center of everything. 

**Whānau** meant not just relatives by blood, but a kinship that bound us to each other and to the **whenua**, the land itself.

We were **tangata whenua**. People of the land. And we knew no other way of living than this: in harmony with Aotearoa, speaking only **te reo Māori**, our language — the language of our **tīpuna**, our ancestors, stretching back through countless generations.

---

### ACT 2: KAI & MAHI
**[1:15 - 3:15] — Tone: Grounded, practical, reverent for the work**

Our survival depended on understanding the land and the sea in ways that went deeper than mere survival. It was a sacred relationship.

[LONGER PAUSE — visual montage begins]

In spring, we cultivated our gardens. The **kūmara** [PAUSE] — the sweet potato brought from Hawaiki by our ancestors — grew in carefully tended soil. We also grew taro, another precious **kai whenua**, food from the land. We understood rotation, soil quality, when to plant and when to harvest. This was **mahi** [PAUSE] — our work — but it was also knowledge, passed from **tūpuna** to **tamariki**, from grandparents to children.

[PAUSE for visual transition]

When hunting season came, our men tracked moa, the great flightless birds, and deer through dense forests. They laid snares, used spears with precision honed over generations. Every part of the animal was used — nothing wasted. The bones became tools. The hides became clothing. Nothing was taken without gratitude.

[PAUSE]

But our most abundant food came from the **moana**, the ocean. We fished with nets woven from native flax. We dove for **kina** and **pāua**. We gathered **pipi** and **tuatua** from the shores. The **kai moana** sustained us. It was our supermarket, our pharmacy, our security.

[PAUSE for visual transition]

And we built homes worthy of our **mahi**. Our **whare** were constructed with precision and care. Wooden frames lashed together with materials from the land. Thatch roofs that shed rain and kept warmth inside. Inside the **whare**, a central **pūtātā** [PAUSE] — a fire — where **whānau** gathered to cook, to talk, to share stories.

Some of us built **pā** [PAUSE] — fortified villages positioned on hilltops overlooking valleys and waterways. 

[PAUSE for visual moment]

These were not just defensive structures. They were centers of community. From the pā, we could see our crops. We could watch for danger. We could gather together.

Every season had its own rhythm. Every **mahi** had its purpose. We lived not just *on* the land — we lived *with* the land, understanding its patterns, its gifts, its requirements.

There was no separation between us and Aotearoa. We were of this place. It was of us.

---

### ACT 3: CULTURE & WHAKAPAPA
**[3:15 - 4:45] — Tone: Spiritual, reverent, authoritative**

To be Māori was to carry your **whakapapa** [PAUSE] — your genealogy — in your heart and on your tongue. 

[PAUSE for visual]

Your **whakapapa** connected you to your **iwi**, to your ancestors, to the gods themselves. To recite one's **whakapapa** was to claim identity. It was to say: "I know who I am. I know where I come from."

The **tohunga** [PAUSE] — our experts and priests — were the keepers of this knowledge. 

[PAUSE]

A **tohunga whakairo** was a master carver. A **tohunga tapua** was a spiritual expert. A **tohunga kōrero** was a keeper of stories. Each had spent years, sometimes decades, mastering their craft and knowledge.

Knowledge in our culture was sacred. It was not written in books — it was carried in memory, in practice, in the careful teaching of the next generation. This is why the **kōrero** [PAUSE] — the talking, the discussion — was so important. 

[PAUSE]

When important decisions needed to be made, we gathered for **hui**. We sat together. We listened. We spoke. We debated. We reached consensus through the power of collective wisdom.

And we expressed our identity through the arts. [PAUSE for visual]

Our **tohunga whakairo** carved intricate patterns into wood and bone. Each pattern had meaning. Each spiral and line told a story or represented a genealogy. The **whakairo** wasn't decoration — it was history frozen in wood.

Our **wāhine** — our women — performed the ancient art of **raranga**, weaving. With flax from our land, they created baskets, mats, and clothing of stunning beauty and durability. Patterns were handed down through generations. Each design carried meaning.

And we gathered to perform the **haka** [PAUSE] — the traditional dance that expressed our power, our unity, our fierce pride as Māori. 

[PAUSE]

The **haka** was not entertainment. It was ceremony. It was the expression of our collective **wairua** — our spirit.

We also sang **waiata** [PAUSE] — our songs — which carried our stories. Through **waiata**, we remembered great victories. We mourned our dead. We celebrated love and **whānau**. The **waiata** kept our history alive.

Our spiritual beliefs ran deep. We believed in **atua** [PAUSE] — gods — who inhabited every part of nature. **Tangaroa** ruled the moana. **Tāne** guarded the forests. **Papatūānuku** was the earth mother. Every mountain, every river, every tree had its own **mauri** [PAUSE] — its own spiritual essence.

We performed **karakia** [PAUSE] — prayers and incantations — to acknowledge these forces and seek their protection. Before we hunted, we performed **karakia**. Before we planted, **karakia**. These were not superstitions — they were expressions of respect and gratitude for the life we took and the gifts we received.

This was our culture. This was our way.

### ACT 4: TE REO MĀORI - THE LIVING LANGUAGE
**[4:45 - 6:45] — Tone: Passionate, contemplative, intimate, powerful**

But at the heart of all of this — [PAUSE] at the very center of Māori identity — was **te reo Māori**. [PAUSE] Our language. 

[LONGER PAUSE for visual]

**Te reo Māori** was not just a way to communicate. It was a way of seeing the world.

In English, we might say "the ocean." In **te reo Māori**, we say "**te moana**" [PAUSE] — and that phrase carries within it centuries of relationship, of respect, of knowledge about the ocean. 

[PAUSE]

When we said "**tangata whenua**" [PAUSE] — people of the land — those three words held an entire philosophy. We were not separate from the land. We belonged to it. We were part of it.

When we spoke of our "**tīpuna**" [PAUSE] — our ancestors — we invoked their presence. They were not distant history. They were with us, guiding us, speaking through us.

[LONGER PAUSE for visual montage]

Our language encoded our values. 

"**Manaakitanga**" means hospitality and care for others — but it goes deeper. It means recognizing the **mauri**, the essential life force, in every person.

"**Kaitiakitanga**" means guardianship of the land — but it's not ownership. It's a sacred responsibility to protect and nurture what has been entrusted to us.

"**Whanaungatanga**" speaks to relationships and kinship — but it extends beyond family. It's about the connections that bind us all together.

Every word in **te reo Māori** carried this depth. When you spoke our language, you were speaking the worldview of your ancestors. You were participating in thousands of years of Polynesian wisdom.

[PAUSE]

Before 1600, every Māori child learned **te reo Māori** as their first and only language. It was the water we swam in. The air we breathed. The voice of our **tīpuna** speaking through us.

We recited **karakia** in **te reo Māori**. We sang **waiata** in **te reo Māori**. We debated in **hui** using **te reo Māori**. We told our stories, our histories, our genealogies in **te reo Māori**.

[LONGER PAUSE]

In 1600, Aotearoa belonged to us. Our **iwi** governed these lands. Our **whānau** thrived. Our culture flourished. Our language filled the air from the northern tip of the North Island to the southern reaches of the South Island.

We were free. We were whole. We were Māori.

**Te reo Māori** was the voice of that world. It was the expression of our identity, our values, our connection to each other and to this sacred land.

[LONGER PAUSE — contemplative moment]

This is the Aotearoa that existed before colonisation. This is the world our ancestors knew. This is the language they spoke. This is the legacy we carry within us today.

Because even though **te reo Māori** was nearly lost, it was never truly silenced. And today, as we revive our language, we reclaim our identity. We honor our ancestors. We speak as our **tīpuna** spoke.

[FINAL PAUSE — let this land]

**Kia ora.** Thank you for listening to our story.

## 🎙️ RECORDING SESSION TIPS

1. **Do a practice read-through first** (don't record yet)
   - Get comfortable with pronunciation
   - Find natural breathing points
   - Identify any tricky sections

2. **Record in small sections** (by act is ideal)
   - Easier to re-do if needed
   - Reduces fatigue
   - Better audio quality control

3. **Monitor your levels**
   - Aim for peaks at -12dB to -6dB
   - Avoid clipping (distortion)
   - Use headphones to check

4. **Take breaks between sections**
   - Vocal fatigue affects quality
   - Rest 5-10 minutes between acts
   - Stay hydrated

5. **Save multiple takes**
   - Label clearly (Act1_Take1, Act1_Take2, etc.)
   - You can choose the best version later
   - Gives flexibility in editing

6. **Clean up background noise** (optional but helpful)
   - Audacity has a noise reduction filter
   - Can remove hum, hiss, or room ambience
   - Enhances final quality
